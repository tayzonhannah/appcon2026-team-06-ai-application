# CASEL 5 Framework — Social-Emotional Learning (SEL)

## Overview

Social-Emotional Learning (SEL) is the process through which children aged 5–12 develop essential life skills, including understanding and managing emotions, setting goals, feeling empathy for others, establishing positive relationships, and making responsible decisions. The Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) framework (casel.org) is considered the gold standard because decadelong meta-analyses demonstrate that evidence-based SEL programs significantly improve academic performance (up to an 11-percentile point gain), reduce conduct problems and emotional distress, and foster long-term mental health. While schools offer structured SEL instruction, parents are a child's primary emotional co-regulators; implementing the CASEL framework at home bridges the gap between theoretical social skills and daily real-world behavior.

## The 5 Core Competencies

### Competency 1 — Self-Awareness

**Definition:** The ability to accurately recognize one's own emotions, thoughts, values, and physical sensations, as well as how they influence behavior and choices.

**Why it matters:** High self-awareness enables emotional regulation, healthy self-esteem, and a growth mindset. Weak self-awareness leads to emotional dysregulation, poor self-advocacy, and explosive outbursts, as children cannot identify internal distress until it manifests physically or behaviorally.

**Signs it is UNDERDEVELOPED:**

* Child cannot name specific emotions beyond generic terms like "mad," "bad," or "fine."
* Child experiences frequent physical complaints (headaches, stomachaches) without an organic medical cause when facing stress.
* Child exhibits low self-reflection, blaming external circumstances or people for all internal reactions.

**Signs it is STRONG:**

* Child can articulate specific internal states: "I'm feeling anxious about the test," or "My body feels tight because I'm overwhelmed."
* Child accurately identifies their personal strengths, limitations, and triggers.

**Parent strategies — Ages 5–8:**

1. **Visual Feelings Wheel/Chart:** Post a simplified emotion chart on the refrigerator and point to specific emotions together during daily check-ins.
2. **Body Scan Checks:** Guide the child to connect emotions to physical cues ("When you get mad, do your hands turn into fists or does your tummy feel tight?").
3. **Emotion Naming in Media:** Point out characters in storybooks or movies and ask, "Look at their face—what emotion are they feeling right now?"

**Parent strategies — Ages 9–12:**

1. **Daily Mood Journal:** Encourage the child to log three primary emotions each day in a private journal alongside a one-sentence trigger note.
2. **Strength Identification Audits:** Regularly discuss personal strengths and areas of growth using objective language ("You are really persistent with puzzles, but rushing math problems causes errors").
3. **Trigger Mapping:** Co-create a list of personal triggers (e.g., sudden noise, rushed mornings, losing a game) to help them anticipate internal reactions.

**In-the-moment parent script:** "I can see your body is tense and your voice is rising. It looks like you're feeling really frustrated right now. Does that feel right to you?"

**Common parent mistake:** Invalidating or dismissing the child's self-reported feelings (e.g., saying "It's not a big deal" or "You shouldn't feel sad about that") instead of validating the emotion first.

### Competency 2 — Self-Management

**Definition:** The ability to effectively regulate one's emotions, thoughts, impulse reactions, and stress responses across different situations to achieve personal goals.

**Why it matters:** Strong self-management fosters resilience, impulse control, task initiation, and distress tolerance. Weak self-management results in explosive behavioral meltdowns, chronic procrastination, and difficulty adapting to changing environments or limits.

**Signs it is UNDERDEVELOPED:**

* Child has low frustration tolerance, quickly throwing, hitting, or quitting when encountering obstacles.
* Child struggles with transition limits, routinely exploding when moving from high-interest to low-interest tasks.
* Child displays extreme impulsivity, acting on immediate urges without pause.

**Signs it is STRONG:**

* Child utilizes self-calming strategies (deep breathing, taking space) when feeling dysregulated before acting out.
* Child can delay immediate gratification in favor of long-term goals or clear expectations.

**Parent strategies — Ages 5–8:**

1. **Stop-Breathe-Think Visuals:** Practice the three-step routine ("Stop your body, Breathe into your belly, Think of a choice") during calm moments using physical play.
2. **Calm-Down Corner:** Establish a cozy space stocked with sensory tools (weighted blanket, stress ball, visual timers) designated for self-soothing rather than punishment.
3. **Turtle Technique:** Teach the child to pull their arms close, take a deep breath, and hold still like a turtle going into its shell when feeling an aggressive impulse.

**Parent strategies — Ages 9–12:**

1. **Regulation Menu:** Work together to design a list of 5 concrete self-regulation options (e.g., shooting hoops, listening to music, box breathing, taking a walk) posted in their room.
2. **Timer-Based Task Chunking:** Use the Pomodoro technique (20 minutes of focus, 5 minutes of break) to lower executive dysfunction friction.
3. **Cognitive Reframing Exercises:** Teach the child to challenge catastrophic thoughts by asking: "Is this a mountain-sized problem or a molehill-sized problem?"

**In-the-moment parent script:** "Your emotional brain is in the driver's seat right now. Let's step away together and pick an option from your regulation menu until your thinking brain is back."

**Common parent mistake:** Trying to reason, discipline, or teach lessons while the child is actively in an emotional flood state rather than waiting for full physiological de-escalation.

### Competency 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

**Definition:** The ability to make caring, constructive choices about personal behavior and social interactions based on ethical standards, safety concerns, and realistic evaluation of consequences.

**Why it matters:** Enables independence, critical thinking, personal accountability, and personal safety. Weak decision-making abilities lead to extreme peer dependency, risky behaviors, and inability to learn from previous mistakes.

**Signs it is UNDERDEVELOPED:**

* Child repeatedly makes the same rule-breaking choices despite receiving predictable consequences.
* Child impulsively blames others for their actions ("He made me do it") and avoids personal responsibility.
* Child cannot evaluate potential risks or outcomes prior to initiating an action.

**Signs it is STRONG:**

* Child considers short- and long-term consequences before taking action.
* Child demonstrates ownership of mistakes and actively works to repair damaged outcomes.

**Parent strategies — Ages 5–8:**

1. **Consequence Branch Mapping:** Draw simple flowcharts ("If I share the toy → Friend plays happily; If I grab the toy → Friend gets mad and leaves").
2. **Structured Micro-Choices:** Offer bounded options daily to build decision-making muscles ("Do you want to do homework before dinner or right after dinner?").
3. **Problem-Solving Roleplay:** Use stuffed animals or puppets to act out common dilemmas and ask the child to guide the characters to a good decision.

**Parent strategies — Ages 9–12:**

1. **The 3-Option Rule:** Require the child to brainstorm at least three distinct solutions to a problem before acting or asking for adult intervention.
2. **Post-Incident Debriefs:** Use non-judgmental reflective questions after mistakes ("What was the goal? What choice did you make? What was the result? What will you do differently next time?").
3. **Digital & Peer Risk Audits:** Discuss hypothetical scenarios regarding peer pressure or internet usage to pre-determine safe boundaries.

**In-the-moment parent script:** "You have a choice to make right now. Option A leads to X, and Option B leads to Y. Which choice supports what you are trying to accomplish?"

**Common parent mistake:** Rescuing the child from natural, low-stakes consequences of their choices (e.g., bringing forgotten homework to school every time) instead of allowing natural learning opportunities.

### Competency 4 — Relationship Skills

**Definition:** The ability to establish and maintain healthy, supportive relationships, communicate clearly, listen actively, cooperate, resist inappropriate social pressure, negotiate conflict constructively, and seek or offer help when needed.

**Why it matters:** Satisfying social connections are a primary predictor of lifelong psychological health and academic engagement. Deficits in relationship skills lead to social isolation, peer rejection, toxic relationship dynamics, or chronic interpersonal conflict.

**Signs it is UNDERDEVELOPED:**

* Child struggles to join play groups, either bossing peers around or withdrawing entirely.
* Child uses aggressive or passive-aggressive behaviors during peer disagreements rather than negotiating.
* Child cannot apologize sincerely or attempt repair after an interpersonal conflict.

**Signs it is STRONG:**

* Child demonstrates active listening, turn-taking, and compromise during play or group work.
* Child seeks resolution during conflicts using clear "I-statements" and maintains long-term friendships.

**Parent strategies — Ages 5–8:**

1. **Social Script Roleplay:** Practice explicit phrasing for joining groups ("Can I play too?"), setting limits ("Stop, I don't like that"), and repair ("I'm sorry I took that, are you okay?").
2. **Cooperative Gaming:** Prioritize board games or physical games that require players to work as a team against a game mechanic rather than against each other.
3. **Turn-Taking Visual Tokens:** Use physical items (a talking stick or token) during family discussions or play to explicitly structure equal conversational sharing.

**Parent strategies — Ages 9–12:**

1. **Conflict Resolution Debriefs:** Guide the child through analyzing social dynamics using the framework: "My Perspective vs. Their Perspective vs. The Common Ground."
2. **Active Listening Drills:** Practice the "Reflective Loop" at home—requiring family members to repeat what they heard before responding during debates.
3. **Boundary-Setting Practice:** Help the child draft and rehearse firm, polite boundary statements to use with peers ("I value our friendship, but I'm not comfortable doing that").

**In-the-moment parent script:** "It sounds like you and your friend have two different ideas right now. What is a solution where both of you get a little bit of what you want?"

**Common parent mistake:** Intervening immediately to solve the peer conflict for the child rather than acting as a mediator/coach who guides the child to resolve it themselves.

### Competency 5 — Social Awareness

**Definition:** The ability to understand the perspectives of and empathize with others, including those from diverse backgrounds, cultures, and contexts.

**Why it matters:** Social awareness forms the foundation of empathy, compassion, cultural competence, and prosocial behavior. Weak social awareness results in accidental offensive behavior, inability to read social cues, and self-centered social interactions.

**Signs it is UNDERDEVELOPED:**

* Child frequently interrupts, speaks over others, or exhibits an inability to read non-verbal body language cues (e.g., missing when a friend is bored or upset).
* Child exhibits low empathy when someone else is hurt, disappointed, or mistreated.
* Child evaluates situations strictly from their own immediate viewpoint, ignoring context.

**Signs it is STRONG:**

* Child accurately identifies how others are feeling based on subtle non-verbal cues and context.
* Child demonstrates spontaneous acts of kindness, inclusion, and consideration for marginalized or struggling peers.

**Parent strategies — Ages 5–8:**

1. **Pause-and-Ponder Story Time:** Pause during books or shows to ask: "Look at their eyes and mouth—how do you think they are feeling right now? Why?"
2. **Community Kindness Missions:** Engage in shared family acts of service (e.g., preparing food for a neighbor, writing thank-you notes) to highlight others' needs.
3. **Perspective Detective Games:** Point out real-world scenarios subtly ("That little girl dropped her ice cream; why do you think she looks sad?").

**Parent strategies — Ages 9–12:**

1. **Media Perspective Audits:** Watch movies or read news stories together and analyze the motives, background, and emotions of different people involved.
2. **Diverse Exposure:** Intentionally introduce books, media, and cultural events featuring individuals from varied socioeconomic, racial, and neurodiverse backgrounds.
3. **The "Walk in Their Shoes" Exercise:** When the child complains about a teacher, sibling, or friend, ask them to argue the scenario strictly from the other person's perspective for two minutes.

**In-the-moment parent script:** "Look at your sister's face right now. What is her body telling you about how she feels about this game?"

**Common parent mistake:** Shaming the child for a lack of awareness ("You are so selfish!") rather than calmly drawing their attention to the social and physical cues they missed.

## Daily SEL Rituals for Families

### Ritual 1 — Rose, Thorn, & Bud Check-in

* **Time:** 10 minutes at the dinner table or during bedtime.
* **Builds:** Self-Awareness, Social Awareness, Relationship Skills.
* **Instructions:** Each family member takes turns sharing three things about their day:
1. **Rose:** A highlight or success.
2. **Thorn:** A challenge, frustration, or mistake.
3. **Bud:** Something they are looking forward to or a new idea.


* **Age Variations:**
* *Ages 5–7:* Use physical pictures or simple thumbs-up / thumbs-down / thumbs-pointing-forward visuals.
* *Ages 8–12:* Elaborate on *why* the thorn was challenging and what core emotion was linked to it.



### Ritual 2 — Evening Feelings Wheel Check-in

* **Time:** 5 minutes before bed.
* **Builds:** Self-Awareness.
* **Instructions:** Keep a printed Feelings Wheel on the nightstand. Parents and children point to 1–2 specific feelings that best represent their internal state that evening and state one sentence explaining the choice.
* **Age Variations:**
* *Ages 5–7:* Use a simplified 6-emotion wheel (Happy, Sad, Angry, Scared, Surprised, Disgusted).
* *Ages 8–12:* Use a full 3-tier feelings wheel with nuanced secondary/tertiary terms (e.g., *Apprehensive*, *Resentful*, *Optimistic*).



### Ritual 3 — The Weekly Family Meeting

* **Time:** 20–30 minutes every Sunday afternoon.
* **Builds:** Responsible Decision-Making, Relationship Skills, Self-Management.
* **Instructions:**
1. **Appreciations:** Every member shares a specific appreciation for another member ("Thank you for helping me with...").
2. **Schedule Review:** Walk through the upcoming week's routines, appointments, and transitions.
3. **Problem-Solving:** Discuss 1 family problem (e.g., morning clutter, chore distribution) using brainstorming where all ideas are logged without judgment before choosing a trial solution.



### Ritual 4 — Perspective-Taking Story Time

* **Time:** 10 minutes during evening reading or after watching a movie.
* **Builds:** Social Awareness.
* **Instructions:** Select a key moment in a story or film. Pause and explicitly analyze a non-main character's perspective by answering three questions:
1. What is this person feeling right now?
2. What do they need or want that they aren't getting?
3. How would I feel if I were in their exact situation?



### Ritual 5 — Daily Gratitude Round

* **Time:** 3 minutes during morning drop-off or breakfast.
* **Builds:** Self-Awareness, Social Awareness.
* **Instructions:** Go around the circle/car and have each person name one specific, non-material thing they are grateful for today and the reason why (e.g., "I am grateful for the sunny weather because I can play soccer at recess").

## When to Seek Professional Support

1. **Duration and Intensity:** Emotional or behavioral outbursts (e.g., extreme meltdowns, severe anxiety, aggression) persist daily for more than 4–6 weeks despite consistent SEL environmental supports.
2. **Functional Impairment:** The child's emotional or social difficulties significantly impair their ability to make or keep friends, participate in classroom learning, or complete routine family activities.
3. **Severe Regression:** The child demonstrates sustained loss of previously mastered developmental skills (e.g., severe bedwetting, loss of emotional regulation tools, language withdrawal).
4. **Safety Concerns & Aggression:** The child engages in self-injurious behaviors, expresses explicit thoughts of self-harm or suicide, or inflicts physical harm on family members, peers, or animals.
5. **Extreme Social Isolation:** The child exhibits complete social withdrawal, severe panic in response to social interactions, or inability to engage in co-regulation with trusted caregivers.
